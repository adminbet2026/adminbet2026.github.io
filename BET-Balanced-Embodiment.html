The approach

What is Balanced Embodiment Therapy?

The idea behind the name, in plain language.

The short answer

Balanced Embodiment Therapy is a whole-person approach to healing, one that treats your mind, your body, and your spirit as parts of a single connected system, because that is exactly what they are. Conversation matters here, and so does everything your body is doing while we talk: the breath you are holding, the shoulders that will not quite drop, the quiet work of a nervous system that has been running overtime for longer than you realized.

The name itself is the promise. Balanced speaks to the equilibrium we are working toward, embodiment to the practice of giving form and language to what you feel, and therapy to the healing that becomes possible once the two are working together.

Why the body belongs in therapy

When something overwhelming happens, your nervous system does exactly what it was built to do: it braces you so that you can get through it. The difficulty is that for many people, particularly those who have had to stay “on” for years at a time, that bracing never fully switches off, and the alarm keeps running quietly in the background long after the danger itself has passed.

This is why you can understand your own story perfectly well and still flinch at a certain tone of voice, and why you can know with complete certainty that you are safe while still carrying a tension you cannot think your way out of. It is also why rest can begin to feel like one more thing you are somehow failing at. Insight belongs to the mind, but a great deal of healing happens further down, in the body that has been keeping score all along.

What it looks like in a session

If you are wondering what working with the body actually involves, you are in good company, and the honest answer is that it tends to be quieter and more ordinary than people imagine. It unfolds inside the same conversation we would be having anyway, and most of it comes down to three movements.

1
Notice

We begin by learning to catch where a feeling actually settles in your body, whether that is the tightness across your chest, the breath you are holding, or the jaw that has been working overtime for years. It is difficult to release something you have never quite located.

2
Settle

From there we build practical skills, drawn from DBT and mindfulness, that help bring an activated nervous system back down again. These are tools you can genuinely use in a meeting, in traffic, or at two in the morning when everything feels louder than it should.

3
Process

When you are ready for it, we go after the root itself using Brainspotting and somatic work, so that the experiences talking alone could not reach are finally able to move through you and out.

Some sessions are almost entirely conversation, and others work more directly with the body. Whichever it turns out to be, you set the pace, and nothing happens before you are ready for it.

Is this evidence-based?

It is a fair question, and it deserves a straight answer. DBT, developed by the psychologist Marsha Linehan, has decades of research behind it and remains one of the best-studied approaches available for working with overwhelming emotions. Mindfulness holds up well too: a landmark review of forty-seven clinical trials, published in JAMA Internal Medicine, found meaningful improvements in anxiety, depression, and stress.

Body-based trauma work is a younger field, and its evidence base is still growing. The first randomized controlled study of somatic therapy for PTSD, published in the Journal of Traumatic Stress, found large reductions in trauma symptoms, and a 2021 research review maps out where that evidence currently stands. Brainspotting is the newest of the tools I use, and I would rather be straightforward about it than oversell it: the early studies are promising, including several comparisons with EMDR, but larger trials are still ahead of us. That is precisely why I anchor the work in the approaches with the deepest evidence behind them.

I bring these together because people do not arrive as single-method problems. What stays constant underneath all of it is the foundation: a licensed clinician, a genuine relationship, and care that follows your goals rather than a script.

Who this is for

Being trauma-informed does not mean that trauma is a requirement. If you think and analyze for a living but feel like something of a stranger to your own body, if you are carrying stress that logic has never quite been able to touch, or if you have done talk therapy before and found yourself hitting its ceiling, then this is work that was practically designed for you.
